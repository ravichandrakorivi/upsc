### Q1. Consider the following statements regarding final goods:

1. A good becomes a final good because of the economic nature of its use rather than merely because of its physical characteristics.
2. A good purchased by a household can be an intermediate good if it is subsequently used for further production.
3. The same commodity can be a final good in one transaction and an intermediate good in another.
4. All durable goods purchased by households are necessarily capital goods.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

<div>

<strong>A.</strong> 1 and 2 only<br>
<strong>B.</strong> 1, 2 and 3 only<br>
<strong>C.</strong> 2, 3 and 4 only<br>
<strong>D.</strong> 1, 2, 3 and 4

</div>

<br>

**Answer**: **B**

> **Explanation:** Whether a good is final or intermediate depends on its economic use. A good used for further production is intermediate, whereas a good purchased for final use is final. Consumer durables are final goods but are not capital goods merely because they are durable.

# Index Numbers, CPI, WPI and GDP

## 1. Index Numbers and Base Year

* An **index number** measures the relative change in a variable or group of variables over time.
* The **base/reference year** is assigned an index value of **100**.
* Therefore, **2024 = 100** means:

  * 2024 is the reference year.
  * The index for 2024 is fixed at 100.
  * It does **not** mean that the price was ₹100.

### How to interpret an index

| Index | Meaning compared with base year |
| ----: | ------------------------------- |
|   100 | Same as base year               |
|   105 | 5% higher                       |
|   112 | 12% higher                      |
|    95 | 5% lower                        |
|    80 | 20% lower                       |

**Formula for percentage change:**

$$
\text{Percentage Change}=\frac{\text{New Index}-\text{Old Index}}{\text{Old Index}}\times100
$$

**Example:**
If CPI rises from 120 to 126:

$$
\frac{126-120}{120}\times100=5\%
$$

So, the price level represented by the index has increased by **5%**.

---

## 2. CPI — Consumer Price Index

* **CPI measures changes in the prices of goods and services consumed by households.**
* It is based on a **basket of goods and services**.
* Different items are given different **weights** according to their importance in household consumption.
* Therefore, a price change in a heavily weighted item has a greater effect on the overall CPI.

### Important distinction

> **CPI is an index of the price level; inflation is the rate of change in that price index.**

For example:

| Year | CPI |
| ---- | --: |
| 2024 | 100 |
| 2025 | 106 |
| 2026 | 112 |

* 2025 inflation relative to 2024 = **6%**
* 2026 inflation relative to 2025:

$$
\frac{112-106}{106}\times100\approx5.66\%
$$

So, the CPI increased in both years, but the **inflation rate fell** in 2026.

### Current CPI series

| CPI series   | Current base year | Main population/coverage | Compiler      |
| ------------ | ----------------: | ------------------------ | ------------- |
| CPI Rural    |    **2024 = 100** | Rural households         | NSO, MoSPI    |
| CPI Urban    |    **2024 = 100** | Urban households         | NSO, MoSPI    |
| CPI Combined |    **2024 = 100** | Rural + Urban            | NSO, MoSPI    |
| CPI-IW       |    **2016 = 100** | Industrial workers       | Labour Bureau |
| CPI-AL       |    **2019 = 100** | Agricultural labourers   | Labour Bureau |

Because they differ in:

* **basket of goods**
* **weights**
* **price stage**
* **coverage**
* **treatment of services**

## 6. CPI, GDP Deflator and Inflation

The **GDP deflator** measures the price change associated with goods and services included in GDP.

$$
\text{GDP Deflator}
=
\frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{\text{Real GDP}}\times100
$$

### CPI vs GDP Deflator

| Feature  | CPI                                        | GDP Deflator                                    |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Focus    | Household consumption                      | Domestic production represented in GDP          |
| Basket   | Relatively fixed consumption basket        | Changes with composition of GDP                 |
| Imports  | Can affect CPI                             | Imported goods are not part of domestic GDP     |
| Exports  | Not directly part of household consumption | Included because they are domestically produced |
| Services | Included                                   | Included if part of GDP                         |
| Main use | Consumer inflation                         | Economy-wide GDP price measure                  |

**Key distinction:**

> CPI asks: **How have prices faced by consumers changed?**

> GDP deflator asks: **How has the price level of domestically produced final output represented in GDP changed?**

---

## 7. Nominal GDP, Real GDP and Base Year

### Nominal GDP

* GDP measured using **current-period prices**.
* It can increase because:

  * production increased, and/or
  * prices increased.

### Real GDP

* GDP measured after removing the effect of price changes.
* It is used to assess changes in **actual output/production volume**.

### Simple example

Suppose:

|                   | Year 1 | Year 2 |
| ----------------- | -----: | -----: |
| Quantity produced |    100 |    110 |
| Price             |    ₹10 |    ₹12 |

Nominal GDP in Year 2:

$$
110\times12=₹1,320
$$

But part of the increase is due to higher prices. Real GDP uses a common/reference price to isolate the change in output.

### Why is the base year important?

The base year provides the **price structure/reference point** used for measuring real changes.

When the base year is revised:

* weights may change
* expenditure patterns may change
* data sources may improve
* methods may be updated
* historical growth rates may be revised

## 9. MCA-21 and Enterprise Approach

### MCA-21

* MCA-21 is a major source of **corporate-sector information**.
* It provides information from companies registered under the Ministry of Corporate Affairs.
* It improved the availability of information for National Accounts estimation, particularly for the **private corporate sector**.

### Enterprise approach

* The enterprise is used as an important statistical unit for compiling economic information.
* It does **not** mean:

  * only manufacturing enterprises are counted
  * only private enterprises are counted
  * GDP is calculated only from company data

**Remember:**

> **MCA-21 = data source**

> **Enterprise approach = statistical/compilation approach**

Neither of these is itself a method such as the **production, income or expenditure method of calculating GDP**.

### 2. More granular price indices for deflation

This is an important concept.

Suppose a manufacturing industry's nominal output is:

**₹1,000 crore**

But prices have increased by 25%. We cannot simply conclude that real production increased by 25%.

We need to **remove the price effect**. This process is called **deflation**.

If we have a suitable price index:

$$
\text{Real Output} \approx
\frac{\text{Nominal Output}}{\text{Price Index}}\times100
$$

The problem is that using **one broad price index** for many different products may not accurately capture their individual price movements.

Therefore, **more granular price indices** means using price information that is more closely matched to:

* particular products
* industries
* activities
* groups of goods

### Simple example

Imagine an industry produces:

| Product   | Nominal value | Price change |
| --------- | ------------: | -----------: |
| Steel     |    ₹600 crore |         +10% |
| Machinery |    ₹400 crore |         +30% |

Using one broad price index for the entire industry could give a less accurate estimate of real output.

Using **separate/more detailed price indices** allows the price effect to be removed more appropriately.

So:

> **More granular deflators → better separation of price change from actual volume/output change.**

---

### 3. Double deflation

This is probably the most important concept in the question.

To understand **double deflation**, first remember:

$$
\text{GVA} = \text{Output} - \text{Intermediate Consumption}
$$

Suppose a factory has:

* Output = ₹1,000 crore
* Intermediate inputs = ₹600 crore

Therefore:

$$
GVA = 1000-600=\ ₹400\text{ crore}
$$

Now imagine prices of both **output and inputs** change.

If we want to calculate **real GVA**, simply deflating nominal GVA using one price index can give a misleading result.

### Double deflation does this:

It separately adjusts:

1. **Output** for changes in output prices
2. **Intermediate inputs** for changes in input prices

Then:

$$
\text{Real GVA}
=
\text{Real Output}
-
\text{Real Intermediate Consumption}
$$

### Example

Suppose nominal figures are:

|                     | Nominal value |
| ------------------- | ------------: |
| Output              |  ₹1,000 crore |
| Intermediate inputs |    ₹600 crore |
| Nominal GVA         |    ₹400 crore |

Suppose:

* Output prices increased by 20%
* Input prices increased by 50%

Then the two components should **not** be deflated using the same price change.

Approximately:

$$
\text{Real Output}=\frac{1000}{1.20}=833.3
$$

$$
\text{Real Inputs}=\frac{600}{1.50}=400
$$

Therefore:

$$
\text{Real GVA}=833.3-400=433.3
$$

The important point is not the exact calculation but the principle:

> **Output and intermediate consumption are deflated separately before calculating real GVA.**

This is called **double deflation**.
