# Global Atmospheric Circulation

## 1. What is Global Atmospheric Circulation?

Global atmospheric circulation is the large-scale movement of air around the Earth that redistributes heat and moisture between the Equator and the poles.

It is primarily driven by:
- Unequal heating of Earth's surface
- Resulting temperature differences
- Resulting pressure differences

Earth's rotation then modifies the movement of air through the **Coriolis effect**.

**Basic mechanism:**

```text
Unequal solar heating
        ↓
Temperature differences
        ↓
Pressure differences
        ↓
Pressure-gradient force
        ↓
Air movement
        ↓
Coriolis deflection
        ↓
Global atmospheric circulation
```

## 2. Why Does Earth Have Unequal Heating?

The amount of solar energy received at Earth's surface varies with latitude.

**Equatorial regions:**

The Sun's rays strike the surface more directly.

Therefore:
- Solar energy is concentrated over a smaller area.
- Heating is relatively strong.
- Air becomes warmer and rises.

**Polar regions:**

The Sun's rays strike the surface at a much lower angle.

Therefore:
- Solar energy is spread over a larger area.
- Heating is relatively weak.
- Air is colder and denser and tends to sink.

Thus:

    Equator → Stronger heating → Rising air

    Poles   → Weaker heating → Sinking air

This unequal heating creates the basic thermal contrast that drives atmospheric circulation.


## 3. Pressure-Gradient Force

Air moves because of differences in atmospheric pressure.

The force that drives air from regions of higher pressure towards regions of lower pressure is called the:

    Pressure-gradient force

In simplified form:

```text
High pressure ─────────────────> Low pressure
               Air movement
```

The greater the pressure difference over a given distance, the stronger the pressure-gradient force.

**Important distinction:**

    Pressure-gradient force initiates air movement; the Coriolis effect deflects the moving air.

## 4. Role of Earth's Rotation

If Earth did not rotate, the atmospheric circulation would be much simpler.

Warm air would rise in the tropics, move towards the poles at high altitude, sink over the poles, and return towards the Equator near the surface.

However, Earth rotates.

Therefore, moving air experiences the Coriolis effect.

```text
Earth's rotation
       ↓
Coriolis effect
       ↓
Deflection of moving air
       ↓
More complex global circulation
```

The combination of **differential heating + Earth's rotation** gives rise to the idealized **three-cell model**.

## 5. Three-Cell Model of Global Atmospheric Circulation

The **three-cell model** is an **idealized** representation of **atmospheric circulation** in each hemisphere.

Each hemisphere contains three major atmospheric circulation cells:

    Hadley Cell → approximately 0°–30°
    Ferrel Cell → approximately 30°–60°
    Polar Cell → approximately 60°–90°

Therefore, the Earth has:

    Three cells in each hemisphere = Six cells globally

**Latitudinal arrangement:**

```text
90°N ───── 
          Polar Cell
60°N ─────
          Ferrel Cell
30°N ─────
          Hadley Cell
 0°  ───── 
          Hadley Cell
30°S ─────
          Ferrel Cell
60°S ─────
          Polar Cell
90°S ───── 
```

The model is **idealized**; actual atmospheric circulation is more **complicated**.

### Q. With reference to the Northeast Trade Winds, consider the following statements:

1. They blow from the subtropical high-pressure belt towards the Equatorial low-pressure belt.

2. Their northeast-to-southwest direction in the Northern Hemisphere results from the Coriolis deflection of equatorward-moving air.

3. They are associated with the Hadley Cell.

**Which of the statements given above are correct?**

<div>
<strong>A.</strong> 1 and 2 only<br>
<strong>B.</strong> 2 and 3 only<br>
<strong>C.</strong> 1 and 3 only<br>
<strong>D.</strong> 1, 2 and 3
</div>
<br>

**Answer:** **D**


**Explanation:**

- Air moves from the subtropical high-pressure belt (~30°N) towards the Equatorial low-pressure belt.
- As this air moves southward in the Northern Hemisphere, the Coriolis effect deflects it to the right, giving it an east-to-west component.
- The resulting winds blow broadly from the northeast towards the southwest and are called the Northeast Trade Winds.
- They form the surface branch of the Hadley Cell.


> **Key distinction:** The term *Northeast Trade Winds* describes the direction from which the winds come; the Coriolis effect explains why *equatorward-moving air* acquires that direction.


### Q. Consider the following situation:

Air moves from the subtropical high-pressure belt towards the Equatorial low-pressure belt in both hemispheres. The resulting winds are known as the Trade Winds.

Which one of the following best explains why the winds in the two hemispheres acquire different directional components?
<div>
<strong>A.</strong> The pressure-gradient force acts in opposite directions in the two hemispheres.<br>
<strong>B.</strong> Air moving towards the Equator experiences Coriolis deflection in different directions in the two hemispheres because of Earth's rotation.<br>
<strong> C.</strong> The subtropical high-pressure belts are stronger in one hemisphere than in the other.<br>
<strong> D.</strong> The Equator receives solar radiation differently in the two hemispheres.<br>
</div>
<br>

Answer: B

Explanation:

In both hemispheres, the **pressure-gradient force** drives air towards the Equatorial low-pressure belt.

However, Earth's rotation causes moving air to be deflected:
- To the right in the Northern Hemisphere → Northeast Trade Winds
- To the left in the Southern Hemisphere → Southeast Trade Winds

Thus, the same basic pressure-gradient force produces winds with different directional components because the Coriolis deflection is different in the two hemispheres.

> **Note:** First determine the direction of movement from the pressure gradient; then apply the hemisphere-specific Coriolis deflection.
